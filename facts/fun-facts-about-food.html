45 Fun Facts About Food

Facts8 min readUpdated 2026-10-29By the AllFun editors

Food is something we deal with every single day, yet it's full of surprises. Some fruits are secretly related to roses, some vegetables are really flower buds, and plenty of ordinary kitchen moments are chemistry in action. Every fact below matches what botanists, food scientists and historians actually know, so you can share them at the dinner table with confidence.

Fruits That Aren't What They Seem

  • Bananas are berries, but strawberries aren't. To a botanist, a berry is a fleshy fruit that grows from a single flower with one ovary and usually holds its seeds inside. Bananas fit that description, while strawberries don't.
  • A strawberry wears its seeds on the outside. Each little speck on the surface is actually a tiny fruit with a seed inside it. The sweet red part is the swollen tip of the flower stem.
  • Tomatoes once went all the way to the Supreme Court. In 1893, the US Supreme Court ruled that tomatoes should be taxed as vegetables because people cook and serve them like vegetables, even though botanists call them fruits.
  • A pineapple is lots of fruits fused together. Each bumpy segment on its skin grew from its own flower, and the segments merge into one big fruit as it develops.
  • Avocados are giant berries. Botanically, an avocado is a berry with one large seed. Its bumpy green skin once earned it the nickname "alligator pear."
  • Every bump on a raspberry is a mini fruit. A raspberry is a cluster of tiny fruits called drupelets, and each one holds its own seed. That's why raspberries feel a little crunchy.
  • Apples float because they're partly air. Roughly a quarter of a fresh apple's volume is air tucked between its cells, which is what makes bobbing for apples possible.
  • Apples, cherries and peaches belong to the rose family. Pears, plums, apricots, almonds and raspberries are relatives too. They all share the same big plant family as the roses in a garden.

Vegetables, Nuts and Seeds

  • Broccoli, cabbage, kale and cauliflower are the same species. Over centuries, farmers bred one wild cabbage plant in different directions, favouring big leaves, tight heads or chunky flower clusters. Brussels sprouts and kohlrabi came from that same plant.
  • Broccoli is a bunch of flowers. Its florets are clusters of unopened flower buds. Artichokes are flower buds too, from a kind of thistle.
  • Peanuts aren't nuts. They're legumes, in the same family as peas and beans. After a peanut flower is pollinated, its stalk bends down and pushes into the soil, where the pod grows underground.
  • Cashews hang from the bottom of a fruit. Each one grows beneath a juicy, bell-shaped fruit called a cashew apple. The shell holds an oil that irritates skin, which is why cashews are always sold already shelled.
  • Sweet potatoes aren't close cousins of potatoes. Sweet potatoes belong to the morning glory family, while regular potatoes are related to tomatoes, peppers and eggplants.
  • Green bell peppers are simply picked early. Left on the plant longer, many varieties ripen to red, yellow or orange and taste sweeter.
  • Every strand of corn silk leads to a single kernel. Each silk has to catch a grain of pollen for its kernel to grow, which is why a poorly pollinated cob ends up with gaps.
  • Carrots weren't always orange. Many early cultivated carrots were purple or yellow. The orange carrots we know today became popular in Europe only a few hundred years ago.

Kitchen Science

  • Popcorn pops thanks to a tiny steam explosion. Each kernel holds a little water. As it heats, the water turns to steam, pressure builds inside the tough hull, and the kernel bursts and turns inside out.
  • Onions make you cry to protect themselves. Slicing an onion breaks its cells and releases a chemical that floats up as a gas and stings your eyes. Chilling the onion first slows the reaction down.
  • Cut apples turn brown because of oxygen. Once the flesh is exposed, natural enzymes react with oxygen in the air. A squeeze of lemon juice slows the browning because the acid gets in the way.
  • Bananas help other fruit ripen. Ripening bananas give off a natural gas called ethylene. Put an unripe pear or avocado in a paper bag with a banana and it will soften sooner.
  • Water boils at a lower temperature on mountaintops. With less air pressure pushing down, water boils before it reaches 100°C, so pasta and boiled eggs take longer to cook at high altitude.
  • Toast browns because of a chemical reaction. Heat makes sugars and proteins react in a process called the Maillard reaction, which creates new flavors and aromas. The same reaction browns cookies, roasted vegetables and grilled cheese.
  • A spin can tell you whether an egg is cooked. A hard-boiled egg spins smoothly, while a raw egg wobbles because its liquid insides slosh around. Stop a spinning raw egg for a moment, let go, and it may start turning again.
  • Old eggs float. An eggshell is covered in thousands of tiny pores. Over time, moisture escapes and air seeps in, so the air pocket inside grows until the egg is light enough to float.

Taste and Flavor

  • Much of what you call taste is really smell. Your tongue senses a handful of basic tastes, but your nose picks up a huge range of aromas. Pinch your nose while eating a jellybean and most of the flavor vanishes.
  • Umami got its name in 1908. Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda studied the savoury taste of seaweed broth and named it umami. Today it's counted as one of the five basic tastes, along with sweet, salty, sour and bitter.
  • Mint feels cold, but it isn't. A compound in mint called menthol switches on the same nerve sensors that detect cold, so your mouth feels cool even though its temperature hasn't changed.
  • Chili heat isn't a taste. The burn comes from capsaicin, which sets off pain and heat sensors rather than taste buds. Birds don't feel it, so they happily eat wild chili peppers and spread the seeds.
  • Chocolate melts just below body temperature. Cocoa butter turns liquid at a temperature a little cooler than the inside of your mouth, which is why a square of chocolate stays firm in the wrapper but goes smooth on your tongue.
  • White chocolate contains no cocoa solids. It's made from cocoa butter, sugar and milk, which is why it has none of the dark color or bitterness of other chocolate.
  • Your taste cells are always being replaced. The cells in your taste buds wear out and are replaced roughly every couple of weeks, so your sense of taste is constantly renewing itself.

Food Through History

  • Tomatoes didn't reach Italy until the 1500s. Tomatoes, potatoes, corn, chili peppers and cacao all came from the Americas and only spread to Europe, Africa and Asia after 1492. Before then, Italian cooking had no tomato sauce at all.
  • Chocolate started out as a bitter drink. The Maya and Aztecs ground cacao beans into frothy, unsweetened drinks, often spiced with chili. Cacao beans were so valued that people used them like money.
  • Ketchup didn't always contain tomatoes. The name probably comes from a fermented fish sauce from southern China, and early European versions were made with mushrooms, walnuts or oysters. Tomato ketchup came along later.
  • Pepper was once valuable enough to pay the rent. In medieval Europe, peppercorns were sometimes accepted for rents and taxes. In English, a "peppercorn rent" still means a tiny, symbolic payment.
  • People were chewing gum thousands of years ago. Ancient people in Scandinavia chewed lumps of birch bark tar, and the Maya chewed chicle, a natural gum from the sapodilla tree.
  • A dentist helped invent cotton candy. In the 1890s, dentist William Morrison and candy maker John C. Wharton built a machine that spun melted sugar into fluffy threads. They sold it to huge crowds at the 1904 World's Fair, calling it fairy floss.
  • Caesar salad isn't named after Julius Caesar. It's usually credited to Caesar Cardini, an Italian-born restaurant owner who served it in Tijuana, Mexico, in the 1920s.

Spices, Sweets and Growing Secrets

  • Vanilla comes from an orchid. The flavor comes from the cured seed pods of a climbing orchid. On many farms, each flower is still pollinated by hand, using a method worked out in 1841 by a 12-year-old boy named Edmond Albius on the island of Réunion.
  • Saffron threads are parts of a flower. Each thread is a stigma from a crocus flower, picked by hand. It takes a huge number of blossoms to make a small amount, which is why saffron is so pricey.
  • Nutmeg and mace come from the same fruit. Nutmeg is the seed, and mace is the lacy red covering wrapped around it.
  • Honey almost never spoils. It's very low in water and slightly acidic, which makes it a terrible home for germs. Archaeologists have even found pots of honey in ancient Egyptian tombs.
  • Cranberries bounce and float. Ripe cranberries have little pockets of air inside. Many growers flood their fields at harvest time so the berries rise to the surface and can be gathered up.
  • Planting an apple seed won't grow the same apple. A tree grown from a seed produces fruit that's different from its parent's. To keep a favorite variety, growers attach cuttings to other trees in a process called grafting.
  • Supermarket bananas are clones. Common store bananas don't have working seeds, so new plants are grown from shoots of existing ones. That makes them genetically almost identical to each other.

Myths we left out

Some food "facts" get repeated so often that they sound true. These popular ones didn't make the cut: - Myth: Your tongue has separate zones for sweet, salty, sour and bitter. Reality: Every part of your tongue with taste buds can detect all of the basic tastes. The famous "tongue map" came from a misunderstanding of an early 1900s study. - Myth: Swallowed gum stays in your stomach for seven years. Reality: Your body can't fully break gum down, but it still moves through your digestive system and out within a few days. - Myth: Marco Polo brought pasta to Italy from China. Reality: Italians were already eating pasta before he returned from his travels in 1295. - Myth: Eating lots of carrots gives you super night vision. Reality: Carrots provide what your body needs to make vitamin A, which helps your eyes work normally, but eating extra won't let you see in the dark. - Myth: Sugar makes kids hyperactive. Reality: Careful studies haven't found that sugar changes children's behavior. The excitement at birthday parties more likely comes from the party itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is a tomato a fruit or a vegetable?

Both, depending on who you ask. Botanists call it a fruit because it grows from a flower and holds seeds. Cooks treat it as a vegetable because it's savoury rather than sweet. Cucumbers, peppers, pumpkins and eggplants fall into the same in-between group.

Why doesn't honey go bad?

Honey contains very little water and is naturally acidic, and bees add an enzyme that produces tiny amounts of hydrogen peroxide. Together, these make honey a poor place for bacteria and mold to grow. Keep the lid on, though, because honey that soaks up moisture can eventually spoil.

What are the five basic tastes?

Sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami. Scientists are still studying whether other sensations, such as a taste for fat, deserve a spot on the list. For a fresh brain teaser every day, try our daily challenges.

45 Fun Facts About History

Facts9 min readUpdated 2026-10-29By the AllFun editors

History isn't just kings, castles and dates to memorize. It's also people playing board games, grabbing a quick lunch, losing their pencil marks to a crust of bread and accidentally inventing things that we still use every day. This page skips the battles and focuses on clever ideas, odd moments and the surprisingly familiar details of everyday life long ago.

Every fact below lines up with what historians and archaeologists actually know. Once you've read all 45, test yourself with our history trivia quiz, or travel back to the dinner tables of the past with our food fun facts.

Daily Life in the Ancient World

  • Cleopatra lived closer to the Moon landing than to the building of the Great Pyramid. The Great Pyramid of Giza was finished around 2560 BC, about 2,500 years before Cleopatra was born. Only about 2,000 years separate her life from the first Moon landing in 1969.
  • Cleopatra was the first in her royal family to speak Egyptian. Her family was of Greek descent and had ruled Egypt for about 250 years. Ancient writers say she was the first of them to bother learning the language of the people she ruled.
  • Ancient Egyptians loved board games. One favorite, called senet, was played on a long board of 30 squares. It was so cherished that sets were placed in tombs, including several in the tomb of the young pharaoh Tutankhamun.
  • Egyptians often slept on headrests instead of soft pillows. These curved stands of wood, stone or ivory cradled the neck and held the head off the bed, and they were probably padded with cloth for comfort.
  • Ancient Romans grabbed fast food from street counters. Many city dwellers lived in small apartments without kitchens, so they bought hot meals at counters called thermopolia. Archaeologists have uncovered dozens of them in the ruins of Pompeii.
  • The word plumbing comes from the Latin word for lead. Romans called lead plumbum and often used it to make water pipes. That's also why lead's chemical symbol is Pb.

Clues From the Distant Past

  • Some of the oldest known musical instruments are flutes made of bone. Flutes carved from bird bones and mammoth ivory, found in caves in Germany, are more than 35,000 years old. People were making music long before they were writing.
  • Teenagers discovered the famous cave paintings at Lascaux. In 1940, four teenagers in southwestern France squeezed into a hole and found cave walls covered in painted horses, deer and bulls. The artwork is roughly 17,000 years old.
  • The first ice skates were made from animal bones. Thousands of years ago, people in northern Europe tied flattened bones to their feet so they could glide across frozen lakes instead of walking around them.
  • One of the oldest leather shoes ever found is about 5,500 years old. Discovered in a cave in Armenia, it was made from a single piece of cowhide, laced up the front and stuffed with grass, possibly to keep its shape.
  • A man who lived over 5,000 years ago was found in the Alps in 1991. Nicknamed Ötzi, he was preserved so well by ice that scientists could study his clothes, his tools and even dozens of small tattoos on his skin.
  • Woolly mammoths were still alive when the Great Pyramid was built. A small group survived on Wrangel Island, off the coast of Siberia, until about 4,000 years ago, centuries after the pyramid was finished.
  • Some of the earliest writing was bookkeeping. Many of the oldest clay tablets from ancient Mesopotamia, more than 5,000 years old, are records of grain, livestock and goods. Writing may have started as a way to keep track of who owned what.
  • The Rosetta Stone cracked the code of hieroglyphs. Found in Egypt in 1799, it repeats the same message in hieroglyphs, another Egyptian script and ancient Greek. Because scholars could read the Greek, Jean-François Champollion was able to decode the hieroglyphs in 1822.

Ancient Inventions and Big Ideas

  • We count minutes and seconds in sixties because of ancient Mesopotamia. Mathematicians there used a number system based on 60. That legacy lives on in 60-minute hours and 360-degree circles.
  • Rome's Pantheon has a hole in its roof on purpose. The round opening at the top of its concrete dome, called the oculus, is the building's main source of light. When it rains, water falls inside and drains away through holes in the floor. The dome has stood for about 1,900 years.
  • There was a vending machine in the first century. Hero of Alexandria, a Greek engineer, described a device that released a small amount of water when someone dropped a coin into a slot. The coin's weight tipped a lever that opened a valve.
  • The ancient Greeks built a gear-driven calculator. The Antikythera mechanism, recovered from an ancient shipwreck in the early 1900s, is more than 2,000 years old. Its bronze gears could predict eclipses and track the movements of the Sun and Moon.
  • Papermaking was perfected in ancient China. A court official named Cai Lun is traditionally credited with improving the process around the year 105, using materials such as tree bark, old rags and hemp.
  • Movable type was invented in China centuries before it reached Europe. Around the 1040s, an inventor named Bi Sheng made individual characters from baked clay that could be arranged, printed and reused, roughly 400 years before Gutenberg's printing press.
  • Paper money began in China. About 1,000 years ago, merchants and then the government in China started using printed paper notes, because carrying heavy strings of metal coins was such a hassle.
  • Kites have been flying for more than 2,000 years. They were invented in ancient China, where early kites were made of silk or paper stretched over bamboo frames.
  • Chess began in India. The game traces back to one played in India around 1,500 years ago. It spread to Persia and then Europe, and its pieces and rules changed along the way.

Clever Ideas and Happy Accidents

  • Eyeglasses were invented in Italy in the late 1200s. The first pairs had no arms to rest on the ears. People balanced them on their noses or held them in front of their eyes.
  • Bristle toothbrushes came from China. By the late 1400s, people in China were brushing with stiff hog bristles attached to handles made of bone or bamboo.
  • People once erased pencil marks with bread. In 1770, scientist Joseph Priestley wrote about a gummy substance from rubber trees that was excellent for rubbing out pencil marks. That's why many people still call an eraser a "rubber."
  • The first stethoscope was a rolled-up sheet of paper. In 1816, French doctor René Laennec felt awkward pressing his ear to a patient's chest, so he rolled up some paper and listened through the tube. The heartbeat sounded clearer than ever, and he went on to design a wooden version.
  • Braille was created by a teenager. Louis Braille, who was blind from early childhood, had worked out his system of raised dots by about age 15. It's still used around the world for reading by touch.
  • The first hot-air balloon passengers were a sheep, a duck and a rooster. In 1783, the French brothers Joseph and Étienne Montgolfier sent the three animals aloft near Versailles to make sure flying was safe. They landed unharmed, and human passengers took to the sky a couple of months later.
  • A melted candy bar led to the microwave oven. In the 1940s, engineer Percy Spencer was working near radar equipment when he noticed that a candy bar in his pocket had melted. He experimented further, starting with popcorn kernels, and helped create the first microwave oven.
  • Penicillin was discovered thanks to a messy lab. In 1928, Alexander Fleming returned from a holiday to find mold growing in one of his dishes of bacteria. Around the mold, the bacteria had disappeared, and that mold gave the world one of its first antibiotics.
  • A walk in the woods inspired hook-and-loop fasteners. In 1941, Swiss engineer George de Mestral noticed burrs clinging to his clothes and his dog's fur. Under a microscope he saw tiny hooks grabbing onto loops, and he spent years copying the idea in fabric.

Firsts of the Modern Age

  • The first photograph took hours to make. Around 1826, French inventor Nicéphore Niépce captured the view from his window on a coated metal plate. It needed an exposure lasting many hours, so only things that stayed perfectly still showed up.
  • The first powered airplane flight lasted just 12 seconds. On December 17, 1903, Orville Wright flew about 37 meters at Kitty Hawk, North Carolina. That's a shorter distance than the wingspan of a modern jumbo jet.
  • Ada Lovelace wrote a computer program before computers existed. In 1843, she published notes on Charles Babbage's design for a mechanical computer, including step-by-step instructions for calculating a series of numbers. She's often called the first computer programmer.
  • Alexander Graham Bell wanted people to answer the phone with "ahoy." Thomas Edison favoured "hello," and Edison's choice is the one that caught on.
  • The first computer bug was a real bug. In 1947, operators of an early computer at Harvard found a moth stuck in one of its parts. They taped it into their logbook as a joke, since engineers already used the word "bug" for a glitch.
  • One of the first webcams watched a coffee pot. In 1991, computer scientists at the University of Cambridge pointed a camera at the coffee pot down the hall so they could see whether it was full before making the trip. It was later put online for the whole world to watch.

Odd but True

  • Oxford University is older than the Aztec Empire. Teaching was happening at Oxford by 1096, while the Aztec capital city of Tenochtitlan wasn't founded until 1325.
  • Some countries skipped whole days when they changed calendars. When parts of Europe switched to the Gregorian calendar in 1582, October 4 was followed directly by October 15. Britain and its colonies made the switch in 1752 and skipped 11 days.
  • Before alarm clocks were common, people paid knocker-uppers. In Britain and Ireland, these early risers walked the streets tapping on bedroom windows with long poles, or even firing dried peas with peashooters, to wake workers in time for their shifts.
  • A few children were once sent by mail. After the US began its parcel post service in 1913, a handful of families mailed young children to relatives because postage was cheaper than a train ticket. Postal officials soon put a stop to it.
  • The Eiffel Tower was supposed to be temporary. It was built for the 1889 World's Fair in Paris with a permit to stand for 20 years. Its usefulness as a giant radio antenna helped save it.
  • The Statue of Liberty used to be brown. It's covered in copper, which was shiny and reddish-brown when the statue was unveiled in 1886. Over the following decades, air and weather slowly coated it in the green layer we see today.
  • Vikings reached North America about 500 years before Columbus. Archaeologists have found the remains of a Norse settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland, Canada, dating to roughly 1,000 years ago.

Myths we left out

Some history "facts" get repeated so often that they feel true. These popular ones didn't make the cut: - Myth: Vikings wore helmets with horns. Reality: There's no evidence they wore horned helmets in daily life. The idea largely comes from costumes designed for operas and artwork in the 1800s. - Myth: Napoleon was unusually short. Reality: He was about 1.7 meters tall, roughly average for a Frenchman of his time. Confusion between French and English measuring units, plus mocking cartoons, helped create the legend. - Myth: People in the Middle Ages thought the Earth was flat. Reality: Educated people in medieval Europe knew the Earth was round. Ancient Greek scholars had even estimated its size more than 2,000 years ago. - Myth: The Great Wall of China is easy to see from space. Reality: The wall is very long but quite narrow and similar in color to the land around it, so it's extremely hard to spot from orbit without help, and it's not visible from the Moon at all. - Myth: Albert Einstein failed maths at school. Reality: Einstein was excellent at maths and had taught himself advanced topics such as calculus by his mid-teens.

Frequently asked questions

Did Vikings really reach North America before Columbus?

Yes. Norse explorers sailed from Greenland and built a small settlement at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland about 1,000 years ago. They didn't stay long, and Europe as a whole didn't learn much about the Americas until after Columbus's voyages in the 1490s.

Why did people skip days when the calendar changed?

The older Julian calendar's year was about 11 minutes longer than the real solar year, so over centuries the dates drifted away from the seasons. Skipping days in one go brought the dates back in line, and new leap-year rules stopped the drift from happening again.

Are these history facts suitable for kids?

Yes. This page focuses on inventions, everyday life and quirky moments rather than wars or disasters, so it's a great fit for classrooms, homeschool lessons and curious kids. Take a break between facts with something from our games and activities.
